# traditions study: where the Sandbox posture comes from

prepared 2026-09-29 for the Sandbox manifesto, from memory. the brief said the network was blocked; one search call was made to confirm, it returned a result, and I stopped as instructed. quotations marked verbatim are ones I am certain of; everything else is a paraphrase and marked so. dates and figures I am unsure of are marked uncertain.

the posture: constructive, warm anarchism; craft as worship; knowledge as armour; autonomy non-negotiable; radical clarity; playful, strange and experimental; blueprints anyone may adopt, adapt or fork; a business in a loose sense that must feed the maker and open livings for others; small steps that change one's own world first and compound. the maker is a monotheist working from the Quran's core message and no prescribed school, and asks why only Palantir gets an ontology framework and harnesses that powerful.

## 1. constructive anarchism

the anarchism that fits Sandbox is the one that builds. its writers share a claim about evidence, not temperament: people already run most of their lives by voluntary cooperation, and the work is to notice it, protect it and extend it.

**Kropotkin.** Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902) argued against T. H. Huxley that cooperation within a species is at least as strong a factor in survival as competition. Fields, Factories and Workshops (1899) argued for reuniting brain work and hand work. lean on: mutual aid as an empirical claim, not a wish; head and hand united as the healthy shape of work. caution: he chose his examples to suit his case; borrow the claim and test it, not the medieval romance.

**Colin Ward.** Anarchy in Action (1973) is the closest book to the maker's posture. its thesis is that an anarchist society, "a society which organizes itself without authority, is always in existence, like a seed beneath the snow, buried under the weight of the state and its bureaucracy, capitalism and its waste, privilege and its injustices, nationalism and its suicidal loyalties, religious differences and their superstitious separatism" (verbatim, from the opening; the image is borrowed from Ignazio Silone's novel The Seed Beneath the Snow). Ward leaned on Gustav Landauer's remark that the state is a relationship between people, destroyed by contracting other relationships and behaving differently (paraphrase of a translation). lean on: anarchism as a theory of organisation, not disorder; existing cooperative practice as the starting point. caution: Ward's gentleness can shade into reluctance to name enemies; Sandbox names harmful defaults.

**James C. Scott.** Seeing Like a State (1998) is about legibility: the state's need to make land, people and cities readable from above, and the damage done when a high-modernist scheme, an authoritarian state and a flattened civil society meet. against it he sets metis, practical, local, adaptive knowledge that cannot be reduced to a rulebook without losing what makes it work. Two Cheers for Anarchism (2012) defends the smallholder and artisan as the material base of independence. lean on: legibility as how platforms as well as states flatten people into what can be measured; metis as what craft carries and slop lacks. caution: two cheers, not three; the state has also guaranteed rights local orders would have crushed.

**David Graeber.** Debt: The First 5,000 Years (2011) argued that everyday communism, the sharing that happens whenever people work together, underlies every economy. Bullshit Jobs (2018) described work the worker privately believes should not exist. lean on: debt's moral language as a trap with sharing underneath; jobs that exist to look like work, which AI is automating in both directions. caution: some of his anthropology is contested; take the questions, cite specialists for facts.

**Elinor Ostrom.** Governing the Commons (1990) answered Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" (1968) with field cases of communities governing shared resources for centuries. her eight design principles, as usually summarised: clear boundaries of resource and membership; rules fitted to local conditions; those affected take part in changing the rules; monitoring by members or people accountable to them; graduated sanctions; cheap conflict resolution; a right to organise that outsiders respect; nested layers for larger systems. lean on: a commons is neither open access nor property but a governed thing with members and rules; monitoring and graduated sanctions keep it alive. caution: "free for everyone" is open access, which is what Hardin was describing. a release anyone may fork is a gift; a community that maintains it is a commons.

**Murray Bookchin.** libertarian municipalism proposed the face-to-face assembly of neighbourhood and town as the unit of politics, confederated upward. lean on: scale by confederation, not growth; the town as where politics is real. caution: he was sectarian and his assemblies assume more meetings than most people will attend.

**how this differs.** destructive anarchism, from the "propaganda of the deed" to the black bloc, treats the present order as a thing to smash and the smashing as the message. punk anarchism is an aesthetic of refusal, useful as a mood and useless as a plan. constructive anarchism says the alternative already exists in embryo and its work is to build, document and federate it. the libertarian right (Rothbard's anarcho-capitalism, Tim May's crypto-anarchy) shares the word and inverts the content: no state so that property and contract can rule unchecked, with mutual aid as optional charity. the constructive tradition treats hierarchy from property as as much a problem as hierarchy from office, and solidarity as the ground of freedom, not its limit.

## 2. convivial tools and appropriate technology

this tradition asks of a tool what it does to the person holding it, and it supplies the vocabulary for "harmful default".

**Ivan Illich.** Tools for Conviviality (1973) named conviviality as the opposite of industrial productivity: autonomous and creative dealings among people and with their surroundings, against conditioned responses to demands made by others and by a manufactured environment (paraphrase). a convivial tool is one anybody can use, as often or as seldom as they wish, for a purpose they choose (paraphrase). the two watersheds: every major institution passes a first threshold when it begins to deliver what it promises and a second when further growth begins to harm the ends it was built for. radical monopoly is one way of doing a thing dominating so completely that the other ways become impossible. Deschooling Society (1971) proposed "learning webs" of skill exchanges and peer-matching. lean on: the convivial tool as the standard for every release; the two watersheds as the lens for AI; radical monopoly as the precise name for the harmful default. caution: Illich's polemic can become a refusal of all institutions and all scale.

**E. F. Schumacher.** Small is Beautiful (1973) founded intermediate technology: tools between the hoe and the tractor factory, cheap enough to own and simple enough to repair. "Buddhist Economics" gave three functions of work: to use and develop one's faculties; to overcome self-centredness by joining others in a common task; to bring forth the goods a decent life needs (paraphrase). lean on: intermediate technology as the model for AI tools between the chat box and the enterprise platform; work judged by what it does to the worker. caution: small is not a virtue in itself; some goods need scale.

**Lewis Mumford.** "Authoritarian and Democratic Technics" (1964) argued that two technologies have run side by side since the pyramids: one system-centred, immensely powerful and unstable; the other human-centred, weaker but resourceful and durable. lean on: the same tool can belong to either technics depending on who holds it; democratic technics is pro-craftsman, not anti-machine. caution: his epochs are sweeping and his late pessimism total.

**Langdon Winner.** "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980) claimed that design choices settle political questions and that some technologies are inherently compatible with particular social orders (a nuclear plant needs a centralised, secretive authority; a solar panel does not). lean on: every default is a political decision made by someone; ask what social order a technology needs to exist. caution: his Robert Moses overpass example has been disputed on the facts (Bernward Joerges, 1999).

**Ursula Franklin.** The Real World of Technology (1989) defined technology as practice, roughly the way things are done around here (paraphrase), and distinguished two kinds; call them whole-process and prescriptive. in the first the doer controls the work from beginning to end. in the second the work is broken into steps, each done by a different person under someone else's control. prescriptive technologies, in her phrase, breed a culture of compliance. lean on: does the tool leave the doer in control of the whole, or slot them into a step; compliance as the hidden product of enterprise software and of most agent harnesses as sold. caution: she did not call prescriptive technologies evil; she said watch what they do to people.

**Neil Postman.** Technopoly (1992) and Building a Bridge to the Eighteenth Century (1999) give questions to ask of any new tool (paraphrase): what is the problem to which this is the solution? whose problem is it? who might be most seriously harmed? what new problems are created by solving this one? who gains special economic and political power? what changes in language does it force? lean on: the questions as the manifesto's checklist; the trade-off frame, that every technology gives and takes. caution: Postman built nothing; his questions are a brake, and Sandbox also needs an accelerator.

## 3. access to tools and personal computing as liberation

**the Whole Earth Catalog.** Stewart Brand's catalog (first edition 1968) was subtitled "access to tools"; its purpose statement began "We are as gods and might as well get good at it." (verbatim). the 1974 Epilog's back cover carried "Stay hungry. Stay foolish." (verbatim). lean on: the catalog as a form, a curated, reviewed list of tools with the reviewer's name attached; "access to tools" as the whole programme in three words. caution: "we are as gods" is the creed the maker rejects. take the form, refuse the theology.

**Ted Nelson.** Computer Lib / Dream Machines (1974, self-published) told readers "You can and must understand computers NOW" (verbatim, from the cover). his Xanadu project proposed unbreakable two-way links and transclusion, the inclusion of part of one document in another by reference with its origin preserved. lean on: the demand that ordinary people understand the machine rather than be managed by it; transclusion as the original design for receipts on the web. caution: Xanadu is the emblem of the vision that never ships.

**Doug Engelbart.** "Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework" (1962) proposed the computer as an amplifier of human capability, and his frame was bootstrapping: use the tools to improve the tools and the group that builds them. lean on: augmentation, not automation; bootstrapping as the shape of a small organisation that builds its own tools first. caution: he assumed trained users, and the industry chose ease of use; decide, tool by tool, how much learning to ask, and say so.

**Alan Kay and Seymour Papert.** Kay's Dynabook (1972) and Smalltalk treated the computer as a medium children could shape. Papert's Mindstorms (1980) set out constructionism: people learn by building things they care about, and Logo gave children a microworld in which to think mathematically. he wrote of the child as epistemologist, a builder of their own theories of knowing (paraphrase). lean on: every person is a theorist of their own knowing, and a tool should respect that; the microworld, a small, complete, forgiving space to think in; the computer as a medium for ideas, not a delivery channel. caution: Logo in schools became a subject with worksheets; institutions absorb the tools meant to change them.

**Bret Victor.** "Inventing on Principle" (2012) argued that a creator needs an immediate connection to what they create (paraphrase), and that a working life can be organised around a principle rather than a career; "Media for Thinking the Unthinkable" (2013) argues that our tools for thought are still paper-shaped. lean on: immediate feedback as the mark of a thinking tool; explorable explanations; a principle as compass. caution: most of his work exists as demonstrations, not installable tools.

**free software and open source.** Stallman announced GNU in 1983, founded the Free Software Foundation in 1985, and the GPL (1989) introduced copyleft. the four freedoms, paraphrased: to run the program for any purpose; to study and change it, which requires source; to redistribute copies; to distribute modified copies. Eric Raymond's "The Cathedral and the Bazaar" (1997) gave "Release early. Release often." and "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow." (both verbatim). lean on: the four freedoms as the floor for any release; copyleft as what keeps a fork open. caution: open source became the unpaid supply chain of the platforms. the maker's need for a living is the unsolved problem inside this tradition, not a betrayal of it.

**the cypherpunks.** the mailing list began in 1992. Eric Hughes's "A Cypherpunk's Manifesto" (1993) opens "Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age." and states "Cypherpunks write code." (both verbatim). lean on: write code, because the reply to a harmful default is a working alternative; privacy as a precondition of autonomy. caution: Tim May's "Crypto Anarchist Manifesto" is the libertarian right in full.

**Aaron Swartz.** builder of Open Library and organiser against SOPA; his "Guerilla Open Access Manifesto" (2008) opens "Information is power. But like all power, there are those who want to keep it for themselves." (verbatim). he was prosecuted for bulk-downloading JSTOR articles and died in January 2013. lean on: the scientific record should not be sold back to the public that paid for it; the builder's reply in tools. caution: the manifesto asks for civil disobedience with real consequences; go there on purpose or not at all.

**local-first software.** Ink & Switch's "Local-first software: You own your data, in spite of the cloud" (2019) set seven ideals, paraphrased: no spinners; work not trapped on one device; network optional; collaboration still works; files outlive the company; privacy by default; the user keeps ownership. lean on: the seven ideals as acceptance criteria for a tool that is not rented; the file as the unit of ownership. caution: local-first solves ownership, not livelihood or discovery.

**Dynamicland.** Victor's Oakland space (about 2017; approximate) is one where the building is the computer: programs are pieces of paper read by cameras and projectors, and people program together at tables. lean on: computing as a communal, physical, visible practice; the program as an object anyone in the room can read. caution: it is a place, not a package, and cannot be forked from a repository.

## 4. craft as devotion

**Ruskin and Morris.** Ruskin's "The Nature of Gothic" (The Stones of Venice, 1853) argued that the roughness of gothic ornament is the visible freedom of the workman. Morris's "Useful Work versus Useless Toil" (1884) held that worthwhile work carries three hopes: of rest, of product, of pleasure in the work itself (paraphrase). "Have nothing in your houses that you do not know to be useful, or believe to be beautiful." is verbatim ("The Beauty of Life", 1880). lean on: the worker's freedom is legible in the work; the three hopes as a test of any job, including the maker's; useful or beautiful, nothing else. caution: Morris's goods were sold to the rich, and he hated it. craft as worship without a livelihood plan ends as decoration for the wealthy.

**the Arts and Crafts movement.** Ashbee's Guild of Handicraft (1888) tried the guild as a living workshop community. lean on: workshop, school and business under one roof; the guild as a path in for newcomers. caution: the products were expensive, the aesthetic became a style, and most guilds failed financially.

**the Shakers.** "Hands to work and hearts to God" is attributed to Mother Ann Lee (1736 to 1784), but I cannot point to a dated primary document, so treat it as attributed and uncertain. on the record: furniture, boxes and brooms of a plainness that became a design language; designs mostly allowed to spread, though some were patented (uncertain on the balance); a celibate communal sect with pooled property. lean on: work as worship on the shop floor; plainness as respect for user and material; sharing designs as the default. caution: it worked because it was a commune with no household economy.

**Yanagi Soetsu and mingei.** Yanagi coined mingei (folk craft) in the mid-1920s. The Unknown Craftsman (English version by Bernard Leach, 1972) argues that the most beautiful objects are ordinary things made in quantity by unnamed craftsmen for daily use, their beauty coming from surrender to a tradition and a material rather than from genius. lean on: beauty of use, things made in numbers for ordinary people, cheap, sound and unsigned; the craftsman freed by the tradition rather than by originality. caution: mingei romanticised rural poverty, was entangled with imperial Japan's view of its colonies, and the unknown craftsman was often unknown because unpaid.

**Christopher Alexander.** A Pattern Language (1977) holds 253 patterns, each a context, a problem and a solution, written so ordinary people could design their own houses. The Timeless Way of Building (1979) names the quality without a name, the aliveness in places that people recognise but cannot define. lean on: the pattern as the unit of a blueprint, with adaptation built in; a language of patterns is a thing a community can extend, which is what "adopt, adapt or fork" means in practice. caution: patterns freeze into templates, and Alexander himself in 1996 doubted their patterns were making life better.

**Richard Sennett.** The Craftsman (2008) defined craftsmanship as the desire to do a job well for its own sake (near verbatim). lean on: craft as a relation to work, not a category of work; the workshop as a social form that teaches by doing. caution: Sennett assumes time; say what "well" means when the clock is running.

**Matthew Crawford.** Shop Class as Soulcraft (2009) argued that manual work makes cognitive demands office work often does not. lean on: work whose failure is visible; the trades as work that cannot be offshored. caution: his politics drift toward a conservative masculinism and a suspicion of intellectual work; Sandbox's craft includes writing and research.

**shokunin.** the Japanese word for artisan carries the sense of a person devoted to a craft for life and bound by it to serve others well. lean on: devotion across a life; obligation to the community folded into the craft. caution: much English use is marketing, and one craft for life may not suit a playful, experimental maker.

**itqan and ihsan.** ihsan is a Quranic word. the Quran commands justice and ihsan (16:90), says God does not waste the reward of anyone who does good work (18:30), and says life and death test which of you is best in deed (67:2). of God's own work it says He perfected (atqana) all things (27:88), the root of itqan. the saying that God loves that when one of you does a work he does it with itqan is a hadith (al-Bayhaqi, al-Tabarani; graded hasan by some later scholars; not Quran, grading uncertain). the definition of ihsan as worshipping God as though you see Him, for though you do not see Him, He sees you, is from the hadith of Jibril in Sahih Muslim; again hadith, not Quran. lean on: craft as worship has direct scriptural warrant, with God's own itqan as the standard and best in deed, not most, as the measure; ihsan as doing well beyond what is required, under a gaze that needs no customer. caution: the two most quoted work-ethic lines are hadith; mark them so.

## 5. the Islamic knowledge tradition as precedent

the maker's tradition holds the oldest working models for what Sandbox wants: open, rigorous, collective research; provenance on every claim; public goods funded to outlast their founders; knowledge given away so that it keeps giving.

**Bayt al-Hikma.** the House of Wisdom of Abbasid Baghdad is usually described as a state academy under al-Ma'mun (ruled 813 to 833). Dimitri Gutas (Greek Thought, Arabic Culture, 1998) showed that the sources for it as an institution are sparse; the name may have meant a palace library, and the translation movement of the eighth to tenth centuries was funded by a whole class of patrons across the city, including Christian translators such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq. lean on: the real precedent is a two-century, multi-faith, multi-lingual research culture sustained by many small patronages, not a monument; translation as the founding act of a research culture, which is what a blueprint for non-experts does. caution: do not repeat the academy myth as fact.

**Ibn al-Haytham.** al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham (about 965 to about 1040) wrote the Book of Optics in Cairo in the 1010s, joining mathematics to controlled experiment with apparatus described so others could rebuild it. in his Doubts on Ptolemy he wrote, in paraphrase, that the seeker after truth is not the one who studies the ancients and trusts them but the one who suspects them, questions what he takes from them, and submits to argument and demonstration rather than to the sayings of a human being whose nature is full of imperfection (paraphrase of A. I. Sabra's translation). lean on: suspicion of authority as the discipline of a truth-seeker, the maker's radical clarity stated nine centuries ago; describing the apparatus so others can rebuild it, which is a blueprint. caution: his doubts were aimed at Ptolemy, not everything; clarity is not contrarianism.

**Ikhwan al-Safa.** the Brethren of Purity were an anonymous group in Basra in the tenth century (dating debated) who wrote fifty-two epistles covering mathematics, logic, natural science, psychology, cosmology and theology. their names are not in the text; a single later report by Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi names a few. their fable of the animals' case against man before the king of the jinn is still in print. lean on: the closest precedent anywhere for a collective publishing a whole encyclopaedia under a shared name to readers who never learn who they are; the friendship model of research, a small circle each bringing a science; the fable as a way to carry a serious argument playfully. caution: they were esoteric, with degrees of initiation. Sandbox's openness has to be added; it is not in the precedent.

**Ibn Khaldun.** the Muqaddimah (1377) introduces his world history. asabiyya, group feeling, is his engine of history: it lets a group take power and decays in luxury, so a dynasty lasts about three generations (uncertain on the exact framing). crafts flourish in settled cities with steady demand, pass from master to apprentice as an acquired habit (malaka), and decay with the city. lean on: cohesion is the scarce input, not money, and a small group with asabiyya outworks a large one without it; the decay cycle as a reason to keep the group small and plain by design. caution: the cycle can become fatalism; use him for mechanisms, not prophecy.

**al-Jazari.** al-Jazari (1136 to 1206) finished his Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices in 1206 at the Artuqid court at Amid (Diyarbakir): fifty devices, with drawings, dimensions, materials and assembly order (Donald Hill's translation, 1974). lean on: playful engineering with serious mechanics inside, "strange and experimental" with precedent; the book as a blueprint set, meant to be rebuilt, not admired. caution: the play was funded by a prince for a court. say who pays for play at Sandbox.

**isnad and ijaza.** an isnad is the chain of transmitters attached to a report: this person told me that that person told them that so-and-so said. transmitters were graded for memory and honesty, and reports as sound, fair, weak or fabricated. the saying attributed to Abdullah ibn al-Mubarak in the introduction to Sahih Muslim, that the isnad is part of religion and without it anyone could say whatever they liked, is a scholar's saying, not a hadith (paraphrase). an ijaza is a teacher's written licence to transmit or teach a text, carrying the chain back to its author. the maker's tool, with its receipts, echoes this: a claim carries who made it, from what, and when. lean on: provenance as a chain of custody for claims with graded confidence, which is exactly what a receipt is; the ijaza as a licence that travels with the text, as a blueprint should carry its lineage. caution: chains can be forged, and grading the chain is not checking the claim.

**waqf.** an endowment: property whose principal is locked in perpetuity and whose income funds a stated purpose, a mosque, a school, a hospital, a library. colleges, hospitals and libraries were waqf-funded, and surviving deeds spell out salaries and duties. lean on: fund the public good from a locked principal so it does not depend on the founder's continued attention; write the deed in public and in detail so the purpose cannot drift. caution: waqfs ossified, were captured by families and were nationalised by modern states.

**sadaqa jariya.** the hadith in Sahih Muslim, in paraphrase: when a person dies their deeds end except three, a continuing charity, knowledge from which people benefit, and a righteous child who prays for them (hadith, not Quran). knowledge that keeps benefiting after the giver is gone is the exact description of a blueprint that forks. lean on: the reason to publish blueprints rather than products, since a product ends and a blueprint continues in other hands; the fork as the intended mechanism of continuation. caution: this is hadith, and the manifesto should say so; the Quranic ground is that good work is not wasted (18:30).

**al-Andalus and Timbuktu.** in Cordoba al-Hakam II (ruled 961 to 976) built a library reputed at some four hundred thousand volumes, a figure from later chroniclers to be read as scale, not count; after his death the chamberlain al-Mansur purged its philosophical works to please the jurists, and in 1499 Cardinal Cisneros had Arabic manuscripts burned in Granada. Timbuktu was a network rather than a university: scholars teaching by ijaza around its mosques, and family libraries whose manuscripts are counted very variously (uncertain). Leo Africanus reported that books sold there for more profit than any other goods (paraphrase); in 2012 and 2013 librarians smuggled much of the collections to Bamako during the jihadist occupation. lean on: family libraries and scholar networks as a durable, distributed research institution with no campus; the book trade as a livelihood for a knowledge culture; knowledge is lost by purge and fire more than by decay, so copies and forks are the defence. caution: Timbuktu was not a university in the modern sense. open knowledge cultures are fragile and were saved, each time, by people who copied and carried books.

## 6. the diagnosis

**attention and steering.** Tim Wu's The Attention Merchants (2016) traces the trade in attention from the penny press to the feed. Shoshana Zuboff's The Age of Surveillance Capitalism (2019) named behavioural surplus, the data beyond what a service needs, taken to predict and shape behaviour. Cory Doctorow's enshittification (coined 2022) describes the platform cycle: good to users, then squeezing users for business customers, then squeezing business customers for shareholders, then collapse; his remedy is adversarial interoperability, the right to build tools that plug into a platform without its permission. Jaron Lanier's Who Owns the Future? (2013) named the siren server, the hub that takes everyone's data. Nicholas Carr's The Glass Cage (2014) described automation's deskilling. lean on: steering is a business model, not an accident; adversarial interoperability as the constructive reply. caution: the critique can slide into moral panic and into believing ad-tech's own claims about how well it manipulates. claim what can be shown: lock-in, extraction and steering by design, not mind control.

**gatekept science.** five publishers accounted for more than half of all papers indexed in 2013 (Lariviere and colleagues, PLOS ONE, 2015). the public pays for the research, the review and the subscription, and the publisher owns the copyright. the replies: arXiv (1991), the Budapest Open Access Initiative (2002), Peter Suber's Open Access (MIT Press, 2012, itself open) with its distinction between gratis (free to read) and libre (free to reuse), and Sci-Hub, started by Alexandra Elbakyan in 2011. lean on: gated knowledge is a business decision with a measurable cost; readable is not reusable, so blueprints must be libre. caution: open access was captured in turn: article processing charges made publishing the gate instead of reading. "open" needs governance or it becomes the next gate.

**the AI moment.** generated text and images at near-zero cost have produced slop. deskilling is playing out in writing, illustration, code and customer work. and the powerful side of the same technology, the part that does days of work rather than paragraphs, sits behind a skill wall: it belongs to those who can wire an agent, who know what a tool call is and how to read a log. the chat box is the gated version; the harness is the real thing. for the person on the wrong side of the wall the tool has passed its second watershed before its first. lean on: the divide is a skills divide before it is a money divide, so the remedy is blueprints and teaching, which is Sandbox's trade; slop is the deskilled product and craft the name of the alternative; a harness is a tool in Illich's sense only when its owner can read and change it.

**the Palantir question.** in plain words: an ontology layer is a map of an organisation written for machines, a list of the kinds of things it has (plants, shipments, patients), the properties each has, the links between them (this shipment left that plant), and the actions allowed on them (approve, reroute, close) with rules for who may take them. Palantir's Foundry sells this as its Ontology, and its AIP product (2023) lets language-model agents act through the same map. an agent harness is the scaffolding around a model: the loop that lets it plan, act and observe; the tools it may call; the memory and context it is given; the permissions and approvals gating what it may do; the logs; and the tests that say whether it did the job. neither is magic: the ontology is a schema plus a permissions table plus connectors, and the harness is a program that calls a model in a loop and checks the results.

why they concentrate power: whoever holds the map holds the switching cost, since leaving means an organisation redescribing itself; the action layer is where policy lives, so a vendor's product becomes the place an institution's rules are encoded, often invisibly; both require expertise the vendor sells as a service (the forward-deployed engineer), so knowledge of how the map was built stays with the vendor. none of this is a technical necessity. it is a business model built around a real capability.

what the precedents teach. schema.org (2011, a shared vocabulary from Google, Bing, Yahoo and Yandex) worked because it was small, useful today and rewarded by search: ship with a use, not a theory. Wikidata (2012, Wikimedia, CC0), a volunteer-edited public knowledge graph, teaches that references on claims and a permissive licence matter more than schema purity. OpenStreetMap (2004) grew its tags bottom-up, by use, with a wiki for conventions: a documented folksonomy beats a committee schema. the Semantic Web (Berners-Lee, Hendler and Lassila, 2001; RDF, OWL, SPARQL) largely failed because publishing structured data was hard and unrewarded, ontologies were designed top-down, trust and spam were unsolved, and plain JSON APIs did the practical work. open harnesses exist (LangChain, AutoGen, CrewAI, OpenHands), and so do open standards for connecting models to tools, such as the Model Context Protocol (Anthropic, November 2024) and Google's A2A (2025). what is missing is the ontology layer for ordinary people and small groups, and the plain-language blueprint that lets a non-expert wire the two together.

a Sandbox version, from the precedents: the ontology is a set of files a person can read and edit; local-first, so the map of your life or your shop never leaves your machine unless you send it; every object and link carries a receipt (an isnad) saying where it came from and how sure you are; licensed so anyone may fork it; actions bound to permissions written in plain language, with the harness's rules and logs belonging to the user; published as patterns so a small group can adopt the part it needs. the honest counter is Ostrom's: Palantir's product is as much the labour of integration as the software, and a public version needs people who do that labour for each other, which is a commons problem before it is a code problem.

## 7. closing: fit, clash, glossary

**five ideas that fit this maker most exactly.**

1. Ward's seed beneath the snow, with Landauer behind it: the good society is already here in how people cooperate, and one changes the world by contracting different relationships, starting with one's own.
2. Illich's convivial tool and two watersheds: a tool is good when anyone can use it for their own purpose, and a technology becomes harmful when its growth serves itself.
3. the isnad joined to sadaqa jariya: a claim carries its chain of custody, and knowledge is given so it keeps benefiting after the giver is gone. this is the maker's receipts and blueprints, from the maker's own tradition.
4. Alexander's pattern language: a blueprint is a context, a problem and a solution, written for people without the expert's training so they can adopt, adapt and extend it.
5. Ostrom's design principles under Kropotkin's mutual aid: the warmth is an empirical claim about how people get by, and the community that maintains a shared tool needs boundaries, changeable rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions and nesting. how a loose business opens livings for others without becoming a charity or a platform.

**three ideas that clash with the maker's stated values.**

1. "we are as gods." the access-to-tools lineage from the Whole Earth Catalog through the Californian ideology carries a self-deifying and market-worshipping strand. the maker is a monotheist for whom craft is worship, the opposite posture: a servant who works well, not a god getting good at it.
2. autonomy as exit. the cypherpunk and anarcho-capitalist strand (Tim May, Rothbard, much of the later open-source right) defines freedom as freedom from obligation. the maker's autonomy is coupled to opening livings for others; it is Kropotkin's autonomy, grounded in mutual aid, not May's.
3. the unknown, unpaid craftsman. Yanagi's mingei, the Shakers' anonymity and the anti-modern romance in Ruskin, Illich and Schumacher picture the maker as poor, nameless and outside the machine. Sandbox must feed its maker, put names and receipts on work. take the humility and the plainness, drop the poverty and the nostalgia, and say plainly that it is a business.

**glossary.**

- mutual aid: the cooperation people already practise to get by, argued by Kropotkin to be as strong a force as competition.
- convivial tool: Illich's term for a tool anyone can use, as much or as little as they like, for a purpose they choose.
- radical monopoly: one way of doing a thing dominating so completely that the other ways become impossible; Illich's name for a harmful default.
- metis: practical, local, adaptive know-how that cannot be written into a rulebook without losing what works.
- commons: a shared resource governed by members under rules they can change; neither open access nor property.
- pattern language: Alexander's form for blueprints, each pattern a context, a problem and a solution, that ordinary people can combine and extend.
- isnad: the chain of transmitters attached to a claim; the tradition's chain of custody for knowledge, echoed by a receipt.
- sadaqa jariya: continuing charity; knowledge that keeps benefiting people after its giver is gone, which is what a blueprint that forks is.
- ontology layer: a machine-readable map of what an organisation has, how things relate, and what actions are allowed on them.
- agent harness: the scaffolding around a language model, its loop, tools, memory, permissions, logs and tests, that turns a chat box into a worker.
