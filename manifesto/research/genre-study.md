# the manifesto as a form: a study for sandbox

a working note for whoever drafts sandbox's founding text. not a template: a map of what the form has done, where it broke, and what a small maker with a warm posture can take from it.

on sources: the network allowed one search, which confirmed the standard english wording of the Bauhaus opening. everything else is from memory. marks: (verbatim) means i am sure of the exact words; (translation) means a standard english rendering of a non-english original, wording varies by translator; (paraphrase) means my own rendering, never to be quoted as the author's; (verify) means i believe it exact but would check before printing. where unsure of a date or figure, i say uncertain.

## 1. the manifestos

### bauhaus manifesto, Walter Gropius, Weimar, april 1919

for: to announce a school folding the art academy into the craft school, with building as the end all arts serve. structure: a page of proclamation under a Feininger woodcut of a cathedral, then a programme of aims, admissions and courses; four pages in all. tense and person: present and future, "we" as exhortation. signature move: the opening, "the ultimate aim of all visual arts is the complete building!" (translation, confirmed today), and the call for a new guild of craftsmen without the class barrier between craftsman and artist (paraphrase). borrow: a creed followed by a timetable so concrete it could be acted on the next morning. avoid: the cathedral register, expressionist and mystical, which the later Bauhaus quietly dropped.

### the founding and manifesto of futurism, F. T. Marinetti, Le Figaro, february 1909

for: to blow up the museum culture of Italy and make speed, machines, youth and violence the new beauty. structure: a prose prologue (a night drive, a crash into a ditch), then eleven numbered points. tense and person: "we", present tense declaring the future. length: about two thousand words in english. signature move: a racing car ranked above the Victory of Samothrace (translation, wording varies), and the ninth point glorifying war as the world's only hygiene (translation). every line is a punch; the enemy (the past, women, museums) is named with contempt. borrow: numbered assertions, each a sentence you could paint on a wall. avoid: nearly everything else. it burns and does not build, and within a decade its energy fed fascism. this is the register sandbox defines itself against.

### dogme 95, the vow of chastity, Lars von Trier and Thomas Vinterberg, march 1995

for: to strip film of the tricks (lighting, props, soundtrack, genre, the director's name) that had made cinema cosmetic. structure: a short preamble, ten numbered rules, each a flat prohibition, then a personal oath. tense and person: rules in the impersonal "must not"; the oath in "i". length: under six hundred words. signature move: the turn from rules to confession, "i am no longer an artist" (verbatim), and the tenth rule that the director must not be credited (paraphrase). borrow: checkable rules. a film either was or was not shot on location; every tenet can be held against the work and broken visibly. also the humility of a vow as against a proclamation. avoid: the deliberate silliness, and rules so specific to one craft they could not travel.

### the gnu manifesto, Richard Stallman, 1985

for: to explain a project already begun and ask for help. structure: a letter with headed sections: what gnu is, why i must write it, how it will be available, how you can contribute, why all users benefit, and objections answered one by one. tense and person: first person singular, present: "gnu, which stands for gnu's not unix, is the name for the complete unix-compatible software system which i am writing so that i can give it away free to everyone who can use it" (verbatim). length: about four thousand words. signature move: argument from conscience, "i consider that the golden rule requires that if i like a program i must share it with other people who like it" (verbatim). borrow: the "i" behind the project, the contribution section, the objections. avoid: the length, and the urge to legislate.

### a cypherpunk's manifesto, Eric Hughes, march 1993

for: to say what a mailing list would do about privacy in the electronic age. structure: short paragraphs, each a definition or a declaration, from what privacy is to what cypherpunks do. tense and person: present, "we". length: about seven hundred words. signature move: "privacy is not secrecy" (verbatim), "privacy is the power to selectively reveal oneself to the world" (verbatim), and the hinge, "cypherpunks write code" (verbatim). it ends on one word, "onward" (verbatim). borrow: value, then what we make, then the making as the argument: the code is published and works or does not. avoid: it speaks to technicians and never to the neighbour who cannot compile.

### the cluetrain manifesto, Levine, Locke, Searls and Weinberger, 1999

for: to tell companies the networked market talks in a human voice and corporate speech no longer works. structure: a preamble and ninety-five numbered theses (after Luther), each a sentence or two. tense and person: present, declarative about markets and companies, becoming a "we" of ordinary people near the end. length: about two thousand words. signature move: "markets are conversations" (verbatim); "hyperlinks subvert hierarchy" (verbatim). its warmth is its refusal to speak in the voice it criticises. borrow: the human voice as subject and method, theses quotable alone. avoid: the flat list; ninety-five is too many to live by, and the later ones scold.

### manifesto for agile software development, Snowbird, Utah, february 2001

for: seventeen programmers agreeing on what they valued across rival methods. structure: one sentence of context, "we are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and helping others do it" (verbatim), four x-over-y pairs, a qualification, "that is, while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more" (verbatim), signatures, twelve principles on a second page. length: under a hundred words. signature move: the pair, a preference without an enemy: "individuals and interactions over processes and tools" (verbatim) does not call processes bad. the others prefer running software to paperwork about it, collaboration to contract, response to plan (paraphrase). borrow: the pair as firm and fair, the brevity, the honest qualification. avoid: its fate. so short and mild that an industry hollowed it out and sold certificates in its name.

### the mozilla manifesto, 2007, addendum 2018

for: to state the principles of an organisation that builds a browser so they outlast the product. structure: a preface, ten numbered principles, a section on who advances them. tense and person: present, impersonal. length: about nine hundred words plus the 2018 pledge. signature move: principle two, "the internet is a global public resource that must remain open and accessible" (verbatim). borrow: the section on who does what about each principle; a principle with no practice attached is an ornament. avoid: the committee cadence. well made, and nobody has quoted it in anger or in love.

### the conscience of a hacker, "The Mentor" (Loyd Blankenship), Phrack, january 1986

for: written after the author's arrest, to say what being a hacker felt like from inside. structure: a confession that turns, at the end, to address "you". tense and person: present, "i", becoming "we" at the end. length: about six hundred words. signature move: "yes, i am a criminal. my crime is that of curiosity" (verbatim); "this is our world now... the world of the electron and the switch, the beauty of the baud" (verbatim, ellipsis original); "you may stop this individual, but you can't stop us all... after all, we're all alike" (verbatim). borrow: the "i" earns the "we" by telling one true story first. avoid: the self-pity; it is a teenager's text.

### guerilla open access manifesto, Aaron Swartz, Eremo, Italy, july 2008

for: to argue that locking the scientific record behind paywalls is theft from the public and copying it back is a duty. structure: diagnosis (who holds knowledge, who is shut out), dismissal of the polite remedies, a call, a closing question. tense and person: present, "we" and "you". length: about seven hundred words. signature move: "information is power" (verbatim), "there is no justice in following unjust laws" (verbatim), and the last line, "will you join us?" (verbatim). borrow: the concreteness of the ask and the courage to end on a question. avoid: the text was used against him at trial; a call to break law is one other people pay for. sandbox builds legal tools and should say so plainly.

### whole earth catalog, Stewart Brand, 1968 to 1972, final issue 1974

for: a way to find and judge tools for living outside institutions; the subtitle was "access to tools" (verbatim). structure: a purpose statement of a few sentences inside the cover, then criteria for inclusion (useful as a tool, relevant to independent education, high quality or low cost, not already common knowledge, easily available by mail: paraphrase), then the catalog. signature move: "we are as gods and might as well get good at it" (verbatim), and the idea that the argument is the tool list. borrow: the criteria. a written test for what sandbox will and will not build is worth more than any adjective. avoid: "as gods" is the seed of the confidence that built the platforms sandbox pushes against.

### computer lib / dream machines, Ted Nelson, 1974

for: to tell ordinary people they could and should understand computers before a priesthood shut them out. structure: a two-sided, self-published, magazine-sized book, hand lettered; "computer lib" is the political argument, flipped over it is "dream machines", about what screens could become. tense and person: present, "you", with a loud "i". signature move: the cover line "you can and must understand computers now" (verbatim) and the coinage "cybercrud" (verbatim word) for the mystification that keeps people out. borrow: the reader addressed as a capable adult, and the two faces: gatekeeping diagnosed on one, an invitation to dream on the other. sandbox's "gated knowledge" is Nelson's target exactly. avoid: the sprawl.

### the port huron statement, Students for a Democratic Society, june 1962

for: to declare a new left for a generation raised in comfort and unease. structure: an "agenda for a generation", a section on values, long diagnostic sections, and a programme. tense and person: present, "we" as a generation. length: on the order of twenty-five thousand words (uncertain). signature move: the first sentence, "we are people of this generation, bred in at least modest comfort, housed now in universities, looking uncomfortably to the world we inherit" (verbatim), and the phrase "participatory democracy" (verbatim). borrow: an opening that admits who the writers are, comfort included; a "we" that confesses its position is trusted. avoid: the length; almost nobody has read it through.

### William Morris, "useful work versus useless toil" (1884) and the lectures

for: to say work should carry hope of rest, hope of a good product, and hope of pleasure in the making, and that most work under capital carries none (paraphrase of his three hopes). structure: lectures to working men's clubs, moving as argument does aloud. tense and person: present, "we", with "i" for his own workshop. length: an hour's talk each. signature move: work against toil, and from "the beauty of life" (1880), "have nothing in your houses that you do not know to be useful, or believe to be beautiful" (verbatim). borrow: the register, angry and kind, about dye, chairs and wallpaper, not abstractions. "craft is worship" has a secular cousin here: art as the expression of joy in labour (paraphrase, "art under plutocracy", 1883). avoid: the medievalism and the length.

### the arts and crafts movement, 1880s onward

for: a reaction, after Ruskin's "the nature of gothic" (1853) and Morris, against machine-made ugliness and a division of labour that made the worker a tool; Ruskin's claim was that it is not the labour that is divided but the men (paraphrase). its manifestos were mostly workshops and guilds (Ashbee's Guild of Handicraft, 1888): objects sold rather than theses printed. borrow: the workshop as the argument; sandbox's text can be short because the tools say the rest. avoid: the unsolved contradiction: its beautiful things were dear and the workers it honoured could not afford them. a manifesto about access must say who can afford the result.

### Ivan Illich, tools for conviviality, 1973 (a book, so its thesis)

thesis: tools pass a threshold beyond which they stop serving people and make people serve them; a good society limits its tools so everyone can use them freely, on their own terms, with the least control by others (paraphrase). he chose "conviviality" to name the opposite of industrial productivity, meaning free and creative intercourse among people and with their world (paraphrase; verify). a convivial tool is one you can pick up and put down without a licence, a priesthood or a monthly fee (my gloss). borrow: the threshold, which lets sandbox refuse a tool without hating it: it has grown past the size at which it serves. and the word "convivial", which carries warmth where "resistance" does not. avoid: the prophetic tone of his later work.

### others closer to this maker's posture

Colin Ward, anarchy in action, 1973. anarchism as a practice already present, "a seed beneath the snow" (verbatim phrase), in allotments, squats, mutual aid, self-built housing; short chapters, each a case, a quiet "i" reporting on "they". behind him, Gustav Landauer (1910): the state is a relationship between people, and we destroy it by entering into other relationships (paraphrase). together they are the theory of rebellion that builds instead of burns. borrow: show the anarchism already present in a shared password or a library card, and say sandbox's tools extend it.

the situationist international, 1957 to 1972. Debord's "the society of the spectacle" (1967): two hundred and twenty-one numbered theses, the first saying that all of life now presents itself as an immense accumulation of spectacles (translation, wording varies); the wall slogan "under the paving stones, the beach" (translation, 1968). borrow: the diagnosis of the spectacle, which describes the feed and the steering algorithm better than most writing since, and the slogan discipline.

the lucas plan, 1976. shop stewards at a military contractor, facing redundancy, drew up a plan for what the firm could make instead: dialysis machines, heat pumps, a road-rail vehicle, around a hundred and fifty ideas (uncertain), with drawings; their phrase was "socially useful production" (verbatim phrase). borrow: the answer to "what would you build instead?" as a list of things with drawings. the strongest refusal is a working alternative.

slow food manifesto, 1989 (Folco Portinari; movement founded by Carlo Petrini, Bra, 1986). a short declaration from diagnosis (the century took the machine as its model of life: paraphrase) to remedy (a firm defence of quiet material pleasure: translation, wording varies). borrow: rebellion stated as pleasure, with a small act as the practice; sandbox's version is a slow tool, kept.

first things first, Ken Garland and twenty-one others, 1964; revised 2000. "we, the undersigned" (verbatim opening), designers refusing to spend their skill on cat food and striped toothpaste (paraphrase of a longer list), a disclaimer that they do not seek to abolish advertising (paraphrase), signatures; about three hundred words. borrow: the signatures and the refusal to overclaim. avoid: it stops at refusal and names no practice.

Elinor Ostrom, governing the commons, 1990. eight checkable principles for anything shared: clear boundaries; rules fitted to local conditions; those affected can change the rules; monitoring by the users; graduated sanctions; cheap conflict resolution; the right to organise recognised from outside; nested layers (paraphrase). borrow: a rule about who may change the rules. "we are a commons" without one is decoration.

Mierle Laderman Ukeles, manifesto for maintenance art, 1969. two named systems, development and maintenance, and a proposal to exhibit her own housework: "i am an artist. i am a woman. i am a wife. i am a mother. (random order.)" (verify). borrow: the honouring of maintenance. a released tool must be kept; the creed should include the boring part.

## 2. anatomy of the form

the skeleton is old: the communist manifesto (1848) already had diagnosis, programme, critique of rivals, and a call, "working men of all countries, unite!" (translation, Moore 1888).

what every one that lasted had:

- a diagnosis of a mechanism, not a villain: corporate voice, paywalls, the threshold, the spectacle. the reader recognises it from their own week.
- a refusal: the thing we will not do, stated so that breaking it would show. Dogme's rules; first things first.
- a creed: what we hold, in a handful of lines. Agile's pairs; Hughes's definitions.
- a practice: what we do on a monday. "cypherpunks write code." gnu's contribution section. the Lucas drawings. the Whole Earth criteria.
- a call to a "you": Swartz's question, Nelson's "you can and must".
- an "i" visible behind the "we": Stallman, Blankenship, Hughes. committee texts are respected and not loved.
- a length that can be read aloud in under ten minutes, and one line that fits on a wall.

the failure modes:

- vagueness: values with no practice attached. this is how Agile was hollowed out and sold back.
- grandiosity: Barlow's 1996 declaration of the independence of cyberspace, addressed to the "weary giants of flesh and steel" (verbatim phrase), was gorgeous and wrong within five years; so Marinetti's hygiene, so Brand's gods. the bigger the claim, the sooner the world disproves it.
- an enemy defined by hate: Marinetti, the fluxus manifesto's "purge" (1963, verbatim verb), the situationist expulsions. hate powers a group for a season and then eats it. sandbox's enemy is a default, a setting, an industry habit, never a person.
- tenets nobody could break: "we value quality" cannot be violated and is therefore worthless. Dogme's rules could be broken, which is why they meant something.
- corporate mush and the brand deck in disguise: a values page with a palette, "our story", and a mission sentence of three abstract nouns.
- punk energy that only tears down: nothing left to do the morning after.
- outlaw glamour: borrowed danger that other people pay for.
- the length trap (Port Huron, Computer Lib) and the list trap (ninety-five theses are a bag, not a creed; past about twelve, nobody remembers).

## 3. rules to hold the draft against

1. declarative present tense. "we build", not "we will build" or "we believe we should build". the future tense is where promises go to die.
2. "we" with a visible "i". the reader learns early that there is one person, a desk, a reason, and that the "we" is an open door, not a headcount.
3. every tenet in three parts: what it refuses, what it does instead, what that looks like in a tool. "we refuse rented tools; we release what can be kept; every tool runs without an account." if the third part is missing, the tenet is not ready.
4. every claim breakable in practice. for each line ask: what would a stranger point at to say we broke this? if nothing, cut it or sharpen it.
5. short lines that survive being read aloud. read it aloud to someone; every sentence you stumble on is too long.
6. concrete nouns: tools, accounts, passwords, files, keys, shelves, the reader's own machine. no "ecosystems", "experiences", "solutions", "spaces".
7. one enemy, named as a mechanism, in one paragraph, then left alone. the rest is about what we make.
8. one practice per tenet that costs something. a rule that costs nothing is decoration.
9. length: five hundred to nine hundred words, read in four minutes. put the rest (the why, the objections, the licence) in a second document, as Stallman did, and link it.
10. no adjectives that praise the writer. "beautiful", "honest", "careful" are for others to say. show the care in a rule instead.
11. one line that fits on a wall, in the first or last three lines.
12. how to end: on the practice or on the call, never on a summary and never on the founder. the last line is short, present tense, and something the reader can do today. a question is allowed; a thank-you is not.

## 4. notes for this maker

### rebellion that stays warm

the register is Ward and Illich, not Marinetti. the difference is direction, not volume: Marinetti's sentences point at what he despises, Ward's at what already works. name harmful defaults as defaults (a setting someone chose, a gate someone installed, a feed someone tuned), never as a conspiracy, and never name a company or a person; a default can be changed, a villain can only be fought. warmth also comes from scale: a library card, a file that opens in ten years, a tool your aunt can run. one joke, and plainness about being one person with a keyboard, beats any claim about a movement. Illich's threshold is the warmest refusal available: not "this is evil" but "this has grown past the size at which it serves you", which lets the reader keep their phone and still agree.

### faith without preaching

name the source once, in the "i", as origin rather than authority: "i am a muslim; this is where it comes from; you need not be one to use any of it." then state every value in human terms that stand alone: honest work, care for the one who will use the thing, time that is short and not to be spent on what does not serve, a thing made well and given away. the Quran's short chapter on time (al-Asr, 103) says in gist that humankind is in loss except those who believe, do good work, and urge one another to truth and patience (paraphrase of standard translations); the Quran's pairing of belief with good work (amal salih) the maker knows better than i do. the hadith that God has prescribed excellence (ihsan) in all things, and the one that God loves a job done well (itqan), are widely quoted; check their grading before printing. the rule: scripture as origin, never as argument. the reader who does not share the faith must be able to agree with every tenet on its own terms, and should be told so in one warm sentence. do not swap "worship" for a secular word to hide it; say it once and then show it.

### "craft is worship" without preciousness

preciousness comes from adjectives and from talking about craft instead of doing it. define it by cost: the work is done as if seen by the one who sees all work, so no dark patterns, no tricks in the settings, no feature that serves the maker against the user, tests before release, an honest changelog, a tool that still runs when the maker is gone. pair the line with a plain object (a hinge, a well-cut key, a page that loads on an old phone), never with anything artisanal; drop "handcrafted", "bespoke", "curated", "intentional". Ukeles is the corrective: craft includes maintenance, and a creed that honours making but not keeping is half a creed.

### "move fast" and "craft" in one sentence

they only fight when speed means big and unfinished. let speed mean small and finished: "time is short, so we ship small things whole." "one honest tool this month, not a suite next year." the Unix habit, do one thing well and make it work with others (paraphrase of McIlroy), is the technical form. speed is respect for the reader's time, finish is respect for their trust; both are care, and the sentence can say so.

### one maker who is also a collective

write "we" for the practice and "i" for the origin, and say which is which. define membership by act, not by joining: whoever uses one of these tools to route around a gate was sandbox for that minute; whoever forks one and keeps it is sandbox for longer. the licence is the membership card, so state its terms in one plain sentence (what may be copied, changed, sold; what must stay open). that is how the collective exists without a mailing list and how strangers who never learn the name still belong. Ward and Landauer are the theory; gnu's contribution section is the one to copy; Ostrom's rule about who may change the rules is the line that makes a fork not a betrayal. avoid "community", "family", "tribe", "movement". "whoever picks this up" is warmer and truer.

## 5. lines to echo and cliches to avoid

ten lines or images a writer could echo (echo means answer in your own words; quote only what is marked verbatim):

1. "cypherpunks write code." Eric Hughes, 1993 (verbatim). the model for "sandbox builds tools": practice as argument.
2. "access to tools." Whole Earth Catalog subtitle, 1968 (verbatim). two words that could sit under sandbox's name.
3. "we are as gods and might as well get good at it." Stewart Brand, 1968 (verbatim). to answer, not echo: a monotheist's one-line reply, given lightly.
4. "you can and must understand computers now." Ted Nelson, 1974 (verbatim). the reader as a capable adult.
5. "a seed beneath the snow." Colin Ward, 1973 (verbatim phrase). the image of what already works.
6. "have nothing in your houses that you do not know to be useful, or believe to be beautiful." William Morris, 1880 (verbatim). for the craft tenet.
7. "will you join us?" Aaron Swartz, 2008 (verbatim). how to end.
8. the state is a relationship, and we destroy it by entering into other relationships. Gustav Landauer, 1910 (paraphrase). the theory of building instead of burning.
9. the convivial tool: one you can pick up and put down without a licence, a priesthood or a fee. after Ivan Illich, 1973 (paraphrase, my gloss).
10. "i am no longer an artist." Dogme 95, 1995 (verbatim), the vow as humility; and "socially useful production", Lucas Plan, 1976 (verbatim phrase), for what gets built instead.

ten cliches of the genre to avoid:

1. "we believe in a world where..." (a wish dressed as a creed).
2. "a spectre is haunting..." (the pastiche is a century and a half old).
3. "the future is..." or "the future belongs to..." (nobody knows).
4. "we are the ..." (identity declarations in place of practice).
5. "disrupt", "revolution", "reimagine", "empower" (the vocabulary of the platforms being refused).
6. "for everyone, everywhere" (say who, and admit who is left out).
7. "join the movement" (a button, not a call).
8. the enemy in capitals: Big Tech, The System (a mechanism named as a monster stops being fixable).
9. "we will not be silent", "we refuse to be complicit" (heat without a task).
10. the closing mission statement with three abstract nouns, and its cousin, the manifesto that ends by thanking the reader.

one last note. the best texts above are the ones where the writing was the smallest part of the work: a catalog, a plan with drawings, a mailing list that shipped code. write sandbox's so that in a year the tools make it look modest.
